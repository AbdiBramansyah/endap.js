Circular motion from x(t) and y(t)

A particle has coordinates

x(t) = xc + R cos(ωt + φ0)
y(t) = yc + R sin(ωt + φ0)

with θ(t) = ωt + φ0. The two plots below use exactly these equations at the same time t.

Change the centre with xc and yc; the path simply shifts.

Why every point lies on the circle

Subtract the centre from the coordinates:

x − xc = R cos θ
y − yc = R sin θ

Square both equations and add them. The cross terms do not appear, and cos2θ + sin2θ = 1:

(x − xc)2 + (y − yc)2
  = R2(cos2θ + sin2θ)
  = R2

This is exactly the equation of a circle with centre (xc, yc) and radius R. It contains no t: every position the particle can ever have is at the same distance R from C. As t increases, θ increases at the steady rate ω, so the particle travels once around the circle in the period T = 2π/ω.

The graphs show the same fact from two viewpoints. On the left, the radius from C to P always has length R. On the right, the horizontal coordinate traces a cosine while the vertical coordinate traces a sine; together they make one complete revolution per period.

Connection to the magnetic-field module. A charge moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field has a force perpendicular to its velocity. The speed stays constant and the direction turns uniformly, producing exactly this circular motion, with R = mv/(|q|B) and ω = |q|B/m.
